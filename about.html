You were not
born who you are.
You became it.

TSG is not a platform. It is a position. A refusal to accept that who you are was decided for you — by biology, by family, by the gaze of others.

"

One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman.

Simone de Beauvoir — The Second Sex, 1949

Beauvoir said it about women. We say it about everyone. Identity is not a given. It is a construction — ongoing, contested, and always in progress.

The Subordinate Gaze is an archive built on that premise. We collect the work of people who are actively constructing who they are — through their answers, their questions, their contradictions.

We don't offer answers. We offer the right prompts.

Who She Was

Simone de Beauvoir

9 January 1908 — 14 April 1986

Simone de Beauvoir was a French philosopher, writer, and existentialist who spent her life doing things women were not supposed to do.

She passed her agrégation in philosophy at twenty-one, coming second in all of France. She then proceeded to spend the next six decades proving that second place had been a clerical error.

She wrote novels, memoirs, essays, and one of the most important books of the twentieth century — The Second Sex (1949) — in which she proposed, with characteristic precision, that womanhood is not a biological fact but a social construction. The book was immediately banned by the Vatican. She took this as a good sign.

She marched, she signed manifestos, she wore her hair up in a way that has since become iconic, she smoked, she travelled, she loved complicated people, and she never stopped writing. She died in Paris in 1986 and is buried next to Sartre in the Montparnasse cemetery, which is either very romantic or very on-brand, depending on how you look at it.

We like to think she would have approved. We also like to think she would have argued with at least half of it.

The Project

Reinventing yourself
more than once before twenty-five.

We don't really know who Simone de Beauvoir would be today, but as girls with unresolved issues and good taste, we like to imagine her in tabi shoes, drinking overpriced matcha, chain-smoking outside a gallery opening and blasting Charli XCX.

This diary is inspired by her philosophy, and by everyone who has tried to reinvent themselves more than once before turning twenty-five. What you're holding explores identity, obsession, performance, power, and the exhaustion of being constantly perceived. It asks what happens when your self is not something fixed, but something you keep performing, editing, and rewriting without permission.

The Subordinate Gaze understands identity as a chaotic construction: unstable, influenced, always becoming between existentialism and rebellion. More than a publication, it's the kind of book you might have found in your mum's old trunk at sixteen, late at night, and thought: so someone else felt like this too.

01

We question the given

Nothing about your identity is fixed. Not your gender, your class, your nationality, your name. Everything you call "you" is a sediment of decisions — yours and others'. We start from there.

02

We build in public

The archive is not a finished product. It grows with every person who contributes. Your response to an assignment becomes part of the collective record — anonymously, permanently, honestly.

03

We refuse comfort

The assignments are not therapeutic exercises. Some of them will be uncomfortable. That discomfort is the point. You cannot construct a self without first dismantling the one you were handed.
